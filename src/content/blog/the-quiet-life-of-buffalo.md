If you travel through the river and coastal parts of Bangladesh, sooner or later you will see a shape in the water that looks like a dark rock — until it slowly turns its head. That's a water buffalo, half-submerged, eyes half-closed, completely unbothered by the world going past.

There is something calming about watching them. Everything else around a village moves quickly — boats, bicycles, people hurrying to the market. The buffalo does not hurry. It stands in the river for hours, chewing, breathing, letting the water do the work of cooling it down.

## Why they love the water

Buffalo spend so much time in rivers and mud for a simple reason: they don't sweat the way many animals do. Their dark skin soaks up the heat, so on a hot afternoon the river is their fan, and a layer of mud works like sunscreen and keeps flies away. What looks like laziness is really just good sense.

## More than a farm animal

For generations, buffalo have been part of life in rural Bangladesh. They have pulled ploughs through heavy, wet fields where other animals struggle, and carried loads on their strong backs. Their milk is rich and creamy, and in the southern char lands it becomes *mohisher doi* — buffalo-milk curd that people travel to taste.

Today machines have taken over much of the ploughing, but the buffalo hasn't disappeared. On the chars and riverbanks you still see herds grazing on open grass, moving together with the same slow rhythm they've always had.

## What I take from them

I think we could all learn a little from the buffalo. Find your river. Stay in it longer than feels necessary. Let the heat of the day pass over you instead of fighting it. Not everything needs to be fast to be strong.
